Chapter 1: Point reactor kinetics

A reactor is critical when each fission leads, on average, to exactly one further fission. The distance from that balance is the reactivity ρ. Measured in pcm (1 pcm = 10-5) or in dollars, where 1 $ equals the delayed neutron fraction β ≈ 650 pcm.

If all neutrons were born instantly, the power would change by a factor of e every Λ/ρ seconds. With Λ around 0.1 ms, a mere +100 pcm would multiply the power by e10 in a single second. A reactor that fast would be uncontrollable. What saves us are the delayed neutrons: about 0.65 % of the neutrons come from fission products that decay seconds to minutes after the fission. As long as ρ < β, those slow neutrons set the pace.

dP/dt   = (ρ - β)/Λ · P + Σ λi Ci
dCi/dt = βi/Λ · P - λi Ci        (i = 1..6)

What to look for

  • Small step (+100 pcm): a small immediate jump, then a slow rise. The period of that rise is given by the inhour equation.
  • Above +650 pcm (1 $): prompt critical. Delayed neutrons no longer matter and the power explodes on a millisecond scale. The calculation stops when the power has grown a billion times.
  • SCRAM: the power falls to around 10 % almost at once, then decays slowly. The decay heat story starts here: the longest-lived precursor group has a mean life of about 80 s.

Delayed neutron data: six-group Keepin data for thermal fission of U-235 (Keepin, Wimett and Zeigler, 1957). Prompt generation time Λ = 10-4 s. Equations are integrated with an implicit Radau method (SciPy).
